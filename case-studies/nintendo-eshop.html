Background information

What's the problem

The Nintendo eShop is Nintendo's official digital storefront for its game consoles. On both versions of the Nintendo Switch, it's where players browse, purchase, download, and update digital games. Over the summer I decided to revisit some of my favourite Switch games, which led me to the homepage of the eShop that led me down a very interesting rabbit hole. I learned that Nintendo's shop had developed a reputation as one of the most frustrating storefronts around, especially when it came to game discovery. With the sheer volume of low-quality and repetitive titles, it had increasingly become the "Wild West" for Nintendo gamers trying to discover new games.

No filter, no reviewsThe Recent section, one of the primary ways users discover new games, offers no filtering options, leaving small indie titles at the mercy of their chronological release order. There's also no review system at all: no star ratings, no written reviews, nothing. A buyer has to go in blind every time.

Flipped assetsNintendo's submission process also lacks effective checks against existing games. That lets direct clones, asset flips, and copied concepts reach the eShop without ever being flagged before release.

Non-stop slopOne account alone has published nearly 80 games since February 2023, feeding a storefront where low-effort releases overwhelm legitimate ones. With New & Recent relying on a flat chronological list, every game gets the same visibility — which makes finding a quality release among the shovelware genuinely hard.

Research

What does content regulation look like on other platforms?

Valve rewrote Steam's AI disclosure rules over the past two years. Every developer now completes a Steamworks Content Survey before release, separating content generated during development from content generated live during play (Video Games Chronicle, Game Developer). The result appears on the store page as a plain label, roughly the same visual weight as a mature-content flag. Disclosing isn't penalized — not disclosing eventually is: a June 2026 update blocks a release outright if a developer skips the required metadata tagging.

One constraint

Nintendo actually used to have a star rating system, and removed it over its own values. Why has never been disclosed, but you can assume it was the same reason YouTube removed the visible dislike count: protecting creators from dislike mobs. Rest in peace to a very short-lived feature. It begs two questions.

How do we leverage the power of user feedback while still working inside Nintendo's expectations?
How do we indicate game quality without a rating system?

Laying it all out

Who should we be thinking of?

I built two personas mapping out their wants and desires. Looking at the AI final boss's motivations helped me decide which parts of the storefront to regulate; looking at the game explorer's told me how to adjust the store design to fit what she's actually trying to do.

Slop Boss Sammy and Gameplayer Gina.

The current system benefits those like Sammy and is built against those like Gina. Evaluating that gap is how I knew what to focus on.

Which experiences need adjustment

01

The AI disclosure process

The process for developers is strict in all the wrong ways — long and tedious where it doesn't matter, and absent where it does. Checks for AI, asset flips, and low-quality flips are nowhere to be found, which is what lets the flood of low-effort work onto the page.

02

The review system

Nintendo users have no way to communicate with each other on the shop at all. That guts quality control, and it's the step with the highest payoff for the least implementation effort.

03

The recent homepage

Recent is the first thing users see when exploring for new games, but its lack of filters and sorting forces players to dig for hidden gems instead of having them surfaced.

Feature prioritization

Flag AI-made content and let players decide what to do with that information. Valve has rewritten its own disclosure rules more than once trying to define the line — a Nintendo version shouldn't pretend that line is simple.
